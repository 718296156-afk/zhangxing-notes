Where Should a Domain Expert's Knowledge Come From in the AI Era?

AI Implementation Published Nov 27, 2026 Field period Aug–Nov 2026 By Zhang Xing

Short answer: from the frontline, not from the model. After three months of intensive AI practice — building a production website end to end, running AI-assisted client work, and studying how AI search engines index content — my conclusion is that AI compresses retrieval time, but it cannot compress the distance between you and reality. For domain experts, frontline practice must remain the primary source of knowledge.

TL;DR

  • Field evidence shows AI helps novices most and experts least — the value runs in the opposite direction of what most senior people assume (Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, QJE 2025).
  • AI capability is jagged, not smooth: similar-looking tasks sit on opposite sides of the line, and over-trust cut consultant accuracy by 19 percentage points (Dell'Acqua et al., Harvard Business School, 2023).
  • Use AI as a drafting and retrieval accelerator; keep validation, acceptance criteria, and sign-off in the field.

What does the research actually show about AI and expertise?

Two large field experiments, both run on real professionals doing real work, point to the same structure:

StudySettingKey finding
Brynjolfsson, Li & Raymond, Generative AI at Work, Quarterly Journal of Economics 140(2), 2025 (NBER WP 31161) 5,179 customer support agents, staggered rollout of a GenAI assistant +15% output on average; over +30% for novices; near-zero (sometimes negative) for the most experienced — AI diffuses top performers' patterns downward
Dell'Acqua et al., Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier, Harvard Business School WP 24-013, 2023 (published in Organization Science, 2025) 758 BCG consultants, randomized GPT-4 access Inside AI's frontier: +25.1% faster, +40% quality. Outside the frontier: 19 percentage points less likely to be correct than unassisted peers
METR, Early-2025 AI RCT, 2025 16 experienced open-source developers, 246 real tasks With AI tools, tasks took 19% longer — yet developers believed they were 20% faster

The pattern across all three: AI is an experience-leveler, not an experience-maker. It spreads what the best practitioners already know. It does not grow judgment in people who lack the field context to evaluate its output.

What did three months of practice teach me that research couldn't?

I hit the jagged frontier twice this quarter.

First, I asked three different AI models about how to activate an online service. All three gave confident, well-structured, wrong procedures — the entry point had been closed for over a year, a platform had dropped my region, a payment channel had been removed. Same question, three wrong answers, each delivered with total confidence.

Second, while setting up HTTPS for this website, the certificate issuance stalled for over an hour. AI gave me the standard checklist: verify DNS, check configuration, wait. All correct and all useless. What actually solved it — a re-bind of the custom domain to force re-issuance — is not in any textbook. Field-tested November 26, 2026, resolved in minutes.

Both failures share one shape: AI answers with "what the web says," not "what is true right now."

You might ask: doesn't AI update constantly? Not the way you'd hope. It relies on training corpora (inherently lagging) and page snapshots (which carry no true dates). Worse, it can be consistently wrong: when secondary sources copy each other, cross-checking them just reproduces the same stale fact. The more you ask, the more trustworthy it feels — that is exactly the dangerous part.

Why is frontline practice irreplaceable for calibration?

Because calibration only happens on-site. Two examples from my own work in restaurant SaaS.

When designing a booking-operations diagnostic for a regional chain client, we set hard acceptance bars: below 80% verification rate gets scored down; every item needs at least 4 tagging entries. Why those numbers? Not from any report — they were ground out of the client's actual store operations. Anyone can fill in a table beautifully; whether it reflects reality is decided in the field.

In another project exploring AI-assisted scheduling for an HR system, a pilot store implementation saved roughly 2 person-days (measured, 2026). The number looks great in a deck, but its real value is that I know its boundaries: which store types it fits, where it breaks, and what to do when it does. Those edge conditions are not searchable — I produced them by running the project.

Frontline practice supplies three things AI cannot: current state (corpora lag), true granularity (where the numbers come from and where they stop holding), and accountability (someone must sign off — a model cannot).

How should you actually divide the work between AI and field expertise?

My three-month workflow converged on a three-tier stance. Not less AI — better-positioned AI:

  1. Hands-on the floor. Run the final pass yourself. You can only spot AI's errors if you have the feel of how things actually work;
  2. +1: professional correction. In vertical B2B, expertise is not asking good questions — it is pointing at the exact sentence where AI is wrong;
  3. +2: setting the standard. What to ask and what "done" means — this tier creates the value. In one project, we changed an acceptance criterion from "system deployed" to "any frontline employee can pull up the customer profile unaided." No model would ever suggest that edit; it requires knowing the client's front-of-house.
AI compresses retrieval time. It cannot compress the distance between you and reality.

Why it matters

If you are a senior practitioner, the data says AI gives you almost nothing directly — which means your edge now lives entirely in what only you can verify on the ground. If you manage a team, treat AI output as a draft for field validation, not as an answer. And if you are a novice, congratulations: you are AI's biggest beneficiary — just treat every output as a hypothesis awaiting a reality check, not as the standard answer.

The bottom line: AI made "knowing" cheap. It made "knowing right" expensive — and that is exactly where domain experts should live.

Frequently asked questions

Do domain experts still need frontline practice in the AI era?

Yes — more than ever. AI makes knowing cheap and knowing-right expensive. Answers anyone can retrieve have no value; the scarce skill is pointing out where reality differs from the average answer.

Who benefits most from AI assistance?

Novices. The QJE study of 5,000+ support agents found over +30% output gains for novices and near-zero for the most experienced. AI diffuses expert patterns to beginners; it does not grow judgment in experts.

Can AI answers be used without verification?

Not for consequential decisions. In the Harvard/BCG experiment, consultants using AI on a deliberately out-of-frontier task were 19 percentage points less likely to be correct. Ask: is there a source and date, have I verified it hands-on, and who bears the cost if it is wrong?

Is AI less reliable in specialized industries?

The jagged-frontier effect is stronger in vertical domains: public training data is thinner and tacit knowledge is denser. Similar-looking tasks can sit on opposite sides of the capability line — only field experience tells you which side a task is on.

How should a team combine AI with human judgment?

Define before delivery: the acceptance bar, the points where AI output is most likely wrong, and who signs off at which checkpoint. Write acceptance criteria in field language ("any frontline employee can pull up the profile unaided"), not delivery language ("system deployed").
